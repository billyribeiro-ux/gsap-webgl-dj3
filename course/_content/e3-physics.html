Motion doesn't have to be hand-authored — sometimes you let physics author it. A rigid-body engine computes gravity, collisions, friction and restitution for you; you just describe the bodies and read back where they end up. Pair cannon-es (the physics) with Three.js (the visuals) and you have a playground that's different every time.

Two worlds, kept in sync

The core idea: maintain a physics world and a visual world in parallel, and copy from one to the other every frame.

  • Describe bodies twice. Each cube is a CANNON.Body (a mass + a collision shape) and a THREE.Mesh (the geometry you see). The engine only cares about the shape and mass; the mesh only cares about looking good.
  • Step, then copy. Call world.step() to advance the simulation by one tick, then for every pair do mesh.position.copy(body.position) and the same for rotation. That single copy is the entire bridge between physics and pixels.
  • Interaction is just more bodies. A click raycasts to the floor and spawns a heavy sphere above that point — the engine handles the rest, knocking the stack realistically. Drag orbits the camera with OrbitControls.
Use a fixed timestep. Physics engines are only stable when stepped at a constant rate — pass world.step(1/60, deltaTime, maxSubSteps) so cannon-es internally catches up with sub-steps when a frame runs long, instead of exploding. In a Svelte 5 app the idiomatic route is @threlte/rapier: you declare <RigidBody> and <Collider> components and Threlte runs the world for you — the same simulation, expressed declaratively. The principle is identical: step a world, sync the transforms.

From here: stack dominoes, build a marble run, add springs and hinges (ragdolls, vehicles), or throw objects with a drag-and-release. Physics turns a static scene into a toy people can't stop playing with — and that playfulness is its own kind of cinematic.
