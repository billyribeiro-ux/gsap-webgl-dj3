Gravity is simple between two bodies and ferociously expensive between many: every body attracts every other, so the cost grows with the square of the count. That O(n²) all-pairs structure is exactly what destroys CPUs and delights GPUs. Here, 4,096 stars seeded as a spinning disk fall into each other's pull and self-organize into the spiral structure of a galaxy — 16 million force calculations every single frame.

The physics, the stability, the look

  • Sum every pull. Each body's thread loops over all others, adding G·direction / distance². Integrate that acceleration into velocity, then position. It's Newton, one line of WGSL, run 4,096 times in parallel.
  • Softening prevents explosions. When two bodies get very close, 1/distance² rockets toward infinity and the sim blows up. Adding a small softening term to the squared distance (r² + ε) keeps forces finite — a standard astrophysics trick. A touch of velocity damping keeps it stable over long runs.
  • Seed for spiral arms. Bodies start in a disk with velocity perpendicular to the centre (orbital motion). Too little speed and it collapses to a point; too much and it flies apart; just right and you get rotating arms. Colour by speed (cool → hot) and additive blending gives the luminous core.
Beyond brute force: the Barnes–Hut tree. Real astrophysics simulations run billions of bodies, far past O(n²). The trick is to group distant stars and treat each cluster as a single mass — a spatial tree (octree) that drops the cost to O(n log n). Building and traversing that tree on the GPU is advanced compute-shader territory, but the payoff is enormous. For thousands of bodies, though, brute force is gloriously simple and runs at silky framerates — proof of how much raw parallel muscle a compute shader brings.
