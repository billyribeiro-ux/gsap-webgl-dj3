Every shader we've hand-written has been a GLSL or WGSL string — powerful, but untyped, un-composable, and locked to one backend. Three.js's answer, now production-ready in r184, is TSL (Three Shading Language): you build shaders as a graph of JavaScript nodes, and the WebGPURenderer compiles them to WGSL on WebGPU — or transparently to GLSL on the WebGL2 fallback. Same visual, authored the modern way.

Why nodes beat strings

  • Backend-agnostic. Write once; TSL emits WGSL for WebGPU and GLSL for WebGL2. You stop maintaining two shader dialects, and your effects run on the ~82% of browsers with WebGPU and everyone else.
  • Composable & typed. Nodes are JavaScript values: sin(uv().x.mul(6).add(time)). You build helpers, reuse fragments, and your editor autocompletes them. time auto-updates; uniforms wire themselves up. No more string concatenation and manual uniform plumbing.
  • It plugs into materials. Assign your node to material.colorNode (or positionNode, emissiveNode, normalNode…) on a NodeMaterial. You can override just one channel of a standard PBR material with a node — TSL composes with Three's lighting, not around it.
The one new ceremony: await renderer.init(). The WebGPURenderer sets up the device asynchronously, so you init it once before the first render and use renderer.renderAsync(...) (or setAnimationLoop) in the loop. TSL is also the gateway to compute in Three — Fn(...).compute(count) runs a node graph as a compute shader, so the GPGPU particle systems we wrote by hand can be authored declaratively. This is where Three.js is heading; everything raw-WebGL you learned still applies underneath.
