In 1986 Craig Reynolds showed that lifelike flocking needs no leader and no global plan — just three local rules per bird: separation (avoid crowding), alignment (steer like your neighbours), and cohesion (move toward the local centre). It's the textbook example of emergence, and it's a perfect fit for compute: launch one GPU thread per bird, let each scan the flock, and complex behaviour falls out of simple math.

One thread per agent, reading the others

  • The loop is the simulation. Each bird's thread loops over the whole flock, accumulating the three steering forces from neighbours within a radius. At 1,500 birds that's ~2.25 million interactions per frame — nothing for the GPU, impossible to keep smooth on the CPU.
  • Double-buffer the state. Every bird must read the same snapshot of the flock, so we read from last frame's buffer and write to a second one, then swap. Update in place and birds would see half-updated neighbours — the simulation would shear. This read-old / write-new discipline is fundamental to all agent simulations.
  • Render straight from the buffer. An instanced draw makes a little oriented triangle per bird, the vertex shader reading each bird's position and pointing it along its velocity. No CPU-side geometry updates — the data never leaves the GPU.
The O(n²) ceiling — and how the pros break it. Every-bird-reads-every- bird is fine into the low thousands, but it grows with the square of the count. To reach hundreds of thousands, you add a spatial grid: a compute pass bins agents into cells, and each bird only checks its own cell and the neighbours — turning O(n²) into roughly O(n). That grid-build is itself a compute-shader exercise (counting, prefix-sum, scatter). The same neighbour-search powers crowd simulation, particles with collisions, and SPH fluids.
