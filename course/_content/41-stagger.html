You met basic stagger in the first GSAP lesson. Now we unlock its real power: GSAP can stagger across a grid, ripple from any origin, and even compute each element's delay from a custom function. This is how teams animate a wall of 48 tiles into a single, organic wave.

The stagger object, decoded

  • each: 0.04 — seconds between consecutive elements. (Use amount instead to spread the total stagger across a fixed time, regardless of count — handy when the number of items varies.)
  • grid: [rows, cols] — tells GSAP the 2D layout so it can measure distances between cells. Use 'auto' to let GSAP infer it from the DOM.
  • from — the origin of the ripple: 'center', 'edges', 'start', 'end', 'random', or an index/[row, col].
Stagger is a function in disguise. You can pass stagger a function (index, target, list) => delay for total control — drive delays from data, distance to the pointer, or a noise field. And every stagger accepts its own ease (via {`{ each, ease: 'power2.in' }`}) that shapes how the delays themselves are distributed — accelerating or decelerating the wave. This is the deep end most developers never find.

Where you'll use it

Card grids and galleries revealing on scroll; navigation menus opening in sequence; letters of a headline (you saw this with SplitText); list items entering; a bar chart growing bar-by-bar. Anywhere there are many of a thing, stagger turns a flat appearance into choreography. Restraint still matters — a 0.03–0.06s each is usually plenty; too large and it feels sluggish.
