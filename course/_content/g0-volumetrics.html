Everything you've rendered in 3D so far has been a surface. But clouds, smoke, fire and fog have no surface — they're participating media, volumes where light is absorbed and scattered as it passes through. We render them by raymarching: step a ray through the volume, sample the density at each step, and accumulate how much light survives. It's the single most cinematic effect in real-time graphics.

Marching through density

  • The density field. There's no geometry — the cloud is a math function. We sample fbm (fractal noise) in 3D, confined to a band, and animate it by scrolling the noise over time. That function is the cloud.
  • The view march. Step the camera ray forward in small increments. At each step, read the density and accumulate colour, while tracking transmittance — how much light still gets through — which falls off by Beer's law (exp(-density · step)). When transmittance hits zero, the cloud is opaque and we stop early.
  • The light march. The magic ingredient: at each view step, march a few more steps toward the sun and accumulate density along the way. That gives self-shadowing — the dark undersides and bright tops that make clouds read as solid, volumetric form instead of flat fog.
It's a quadratic cost — budget carefully. Every view step does a whole light march, so cost is steps × light-steps per pixel. Keep both counts as low as the look allows, cap the pixel ratio, and add early-out when the ray is fully absorbed. Real productions add a phase function (Henyey–Greenstein) for forward-scattering glow, blue-noise dithering to hide step banding, and temporal reprojection to amortise across frames. But the core loop — march, sample density, march to light, accumulate — is exactly this. You're now rendering the air itself.
