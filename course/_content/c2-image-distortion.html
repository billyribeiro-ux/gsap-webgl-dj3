This is the effect that announces "this site has a budget." A full-bleed plane runs a fragment shader; as the pointer moves, the surface bends toward it like a lens, and the color channels split apart at the disturbance. In production it's usually applied to an image (hover a thumbnail, it ripples) — here we distort a procedural aurora so it runs with zero assets, but the technique is identical.

The three forces in the shader

  • Influence falloff. smoothstep(0.5, 0.0, distanceToMouse) gives a value that's 1 at the cursor and fades to 0 nearby — every distortion is multiplied by this, so the effect is local, not global. Multiply by an eased uHover and it also ramps in/out as the pointer enters and leaves.
  • The lens. Pulling the sampled UV toward the mouse (uv -= (uv - mouse) * influence) magnifies the surface there, like a drop of water sitting on it. Add a sin() term along the radial direction and you get rings rippling outward.
  • Chromatic aberration. Sample the field three times — red shifted one way, blue the other, green centered — and recombine. The offset grows with influence, so the channels tear apart right under the cursor. This is the same RGB-split idea from the glitch lesson, now per-pixel and continuous.
Ease the hover, don't snap it. The single biggest tell of amateur WebGL is distortion that pops on and off. We keep a uHover uniform and each frame nudge it toward its target (+= (target - current) * 0.08) — a one-line lerp that makes the effect breathe in when you arrive and fade out when you leave. For real images, swap the procedural field() for texture2D(uTexture, uv) and pass a displacement/noise map as a second texture to drive the warp.

This is the foundation of WebGL "image transitions" — hover effects, slideshow crossfades that melt one photo into the next, and full-page warp transitions between routes. Once you can displace UVs by a field and split channels, an enormous slice of award-winning web visuals is within reach.
