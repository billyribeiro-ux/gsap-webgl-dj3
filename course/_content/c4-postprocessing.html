A film is colour-graded after it's shot; great 3D on the web is graded after it's rendered. Postprocessing takes the rendered frame and pushes it through a stack of full-screen shader passes before it hits the canvas. Three.js gives us EffectComposer to chain them. Two passes do most of the cinematic heavy lifting: bloom (glow) and chromatic aberration (lens colour-fringing).

The composer pipeline

A composer is an ordered list of passes. The frame flows through each in turn; the last one draws to the screen:

  • RenderPass — renders your scene into a texture (the starting frame). Always first.
  • UnrealBloomPass — finds the brightest pixels, blurs them, and adds them back, so anything emissive glows. The knot here is nearly black with a bright emissive colour, so only the glow blooms. Its key controls are strength, radius and threshold.
  • A custom ShaderPass — our chromatic-aberration shader, sampling the composited frame three times with red/blue offsets that grow toward the edges. This is how you write your own grade: any full-screen effect is just a fragment shader with a tDiffuse input (the previous pass's output).
Bloom only blooms bright pixels. The whole look depends on contrast: a dark scene with a few very bright (emissive, or HDR) elements. If you bloom an evenly-lit scene, everything turns to mush. Set a sensible threshold so only the highlights glow, and pair it with a low-key background. Same discipline as cinematography — bloom is your practical lights, not a flood. In Threlte, @threlte/extras wraps these passes as <Bloom> components so you compose the grade declaratively.

Other passes you'll reach for: depth of field (cinematic focus), film grain and vignette (texture and mood), SSAO (contact shadows for realism), and god-rays. Stack a few with restraint and a simple scene starts to look like a render from a studio.
