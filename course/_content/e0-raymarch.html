Everything you've rendered so far used geometry — vertices and triangles. Raymarching throws that away. The entire scene is described by a math function (a signed distance field): give it a point in space and it returns the distance to the nearest surface. To render, we shoot a ray from the camera through each pixel and repeatedly step forward by that distance — when it's near zero, we've hit something. It's how Shadertoy makes infinite worlds in a few hundred characters.

Why this is magic

  • The SDF is the scene. sdSphere returns length(p) - radius; sdTorus a ring; and you combine them with min(). Swap min for smin (smooth-min) and surfaces fuse like mercury — that organic melting is impossible with regular polygon meshes.
  • Normals come from the gradient. There are no vertex normals, so we sample the SDF four times around the hit point and difference them. That gradient is the surface normal — instant lighting for any shape, no matter how it morphs.
  • The march is the render. A loop of ~90 steps per pixel finds the surface. Because it's all distance math, you get soft shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections and infinite repetition almost for free — just more lines in the same shader.
Raymarching is fill-rate hungry. Every pixel runs the whole march loop, so cost scales with resolution and step count — full-screen 4K raymarching can melt a laptop. Cap the device-pixel-ratio (we use 1.75), keep the step count as low as the scene allows, and add an early-out when the ray escapes. It's a different performance model from mesh rendering: pixels are expensive, "geometry" is free.

This is the gateway to volumetric clouds, fractals (the Mandelbulb is one SDF), glowing energy fields and morphing liquid logos — visuals that simply don't exist in the triangle world. Learn to think in distance fields and a whole dimension of the medium opens up.
