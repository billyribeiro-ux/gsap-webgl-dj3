In 1981, two Disney veterans published The Illusion of Life and named the twelve principles behind every believable animation. Nearly a century later they still decide whether your UI feels premium or amateur. We won't cover all twelve in equal depth, but four of them do 90% of the work on the web. Master these and everything else in this course clicks into place.

1 · Easing (slow in, slow out)

The single highest-leverage idea in motion design. Real objects have mass; they accelerate and decelerate. Linear motion — constant speed — is the dead giveaway of a lazy animation. Press Race below and watch the same distance travelled over the same duration. Your eye instantly trusts the eased pucks and distrusts the linear one.

Live demo
Rule of thumb. For things entering the screen, use an "out" ease (fast start, gentle settle) like cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1). For things leaving, use an "in" ease. For playful, physical UI, a slight overshoot (a spring) adds life — but use it sparingly.

2 · Anticipation & Follow-through

Before a character jumps, it crouches. That little wind-up — anticipation — tells your brain something is about to happen. Its sibling, follow-through, is the settle after arrival: a button that nudges 2 pixels past its target and eases back feels tactile and real. The spring easing in the demo above is follow-through, encoded as a curve.

3 · Staging (guide the eye)

Every shot in a film directs your attention to one thing. Interfaces are no different. Stagger — offsetting the start of many animations — is how we stage motion: instead of ten cards appearing at once, they cascade, and your eye follows the wave. You saw this on the welcome page; we'll automate it with GSAP later.

4 · Timing (the feel of weight)

Duration communicates mass. Heavy things move slowly; light things snap. Most UI motion lives between 150ms and 600ms. Under 100ms feels instant (good for hovers); over 800ms feels sluggish (reserve it for grand, hero-scale reveals). When in doubt, make it faster — users feel speed as quality.

The cardinal sin: animating the wrong properties. Animate transform and opacity — the browser hands those to the GPU and they cost almost nothing. Animating width, top, or box-shadow forces expensive layout/paint on every frame and tanks your framerate. We'll prove this in the performance lesson.
