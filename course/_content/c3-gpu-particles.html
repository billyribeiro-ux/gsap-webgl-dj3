Particle systems look expensive, and done naively they are — animating 6,000 objects on the CPU will crawl. The professional approach flips it: give every particle two positions (shape A and shape B) as vertex attributes, then mix between them in the vertex shader with one uniform. The GPU morphs all 6,000 in parallel, essentially for free.

Why this is fast

  • The morph lives in the shader. Each vertex carries aA (a point on a sphere) and aB (a point on a torus knot). The vertex shader computes mix(aA, aB, uMorph). Change the single uMorph uniform and every particle moves — no JavaScript loop over points, no buffer rewrites per frame.
  • Even distribution matters. The sphere uses a Fibonacci spiral so points spread evenly instead of bunching at the poles. Good source distributions are what make particle shapes read clearly.
  • Additive blending = glow. With blending: AdditiveBlending and depthWrite: false, overlapping particles sum their light, giving that luminous, nebula quality. The fragment shader discards pixels outside a circle so each point is a soft round dot, not a square.
Any two point sets can morph. The shapes are just arrays of positions, so aB could be the vertices sampled from a 3D model, or — the showstopper — points sampled from text rendered to a canvas. That's how those "particles assemble into a word, then scatter" hero animations work: sample a word into a position buffer and morph the sphere into it. You now have the entire mechanism.
