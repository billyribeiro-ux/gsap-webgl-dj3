FLIP is a four-letter technique — First, Last, Invert, Play — and one of the most satisfying effects on the web. Measure where each element starts (First), let it jump to its new position (Last), invert that jump with a transform so it appears not to have moved, then animate the transform away (Play). Doing this by hand is fiddly. Svelte's animate:flip does all of it.

Reorder without the teleport

Hit Shuffle or Sort A–Z. Every chip smoothly glides to its new slot instead of snapping. The list is just an array — we reorder it, and FLIP animates the consequences.

The key is everything. animate:flip only works in a keyed each block — {#each items as item (item)}. The key is the stable identity Svelte uses to recognize "this is the same chip, it just moved." Use a unique, stable value (an id, or the text if unique). Use the array index as a key and FLIP breaks — because the index never "moves," every position looks occupied by a different item.

Enter, move, and leave: combine with transitions

animate:flip handles items that move. Pair it with transition: for items that enter or leave, and you get fully fluid lists — add an item and it flies in while the others slide to make room; remove one and it fades while the gap closes. For the classic "item moves from list A to list B" effect (think dragging a card between columns), Svelte's crossfade transition sends a matched pair: the element fades out of one list and a twin fades into the other at the right place. Together, flip + crossfade are how you build kanban boards, sortable galleries and filterable grids that feel physical.

Why this matters beyond looks

Motion here isn't decoration — it's continuity. When items glide instead of teleporting, the user's eye tracks where their item went. Sort a list and they can follow their favorite to its new rank; filter a grid and they see what left versus what simply moved. FLIP preserves the user's mental model through change, which is the highest purpose of interface animation.
