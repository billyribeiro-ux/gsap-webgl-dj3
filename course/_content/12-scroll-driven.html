This is one of the most important additions to CSS in a decade. Scroll-driven animations let a regular @keyframes animation be advanced by scroll position instead of time. The work runs on the compositor thread, so even a long list of reveals stays at 60fps on a budget phone — something a JavaScript scroll handler can never guarantee.

Reveal on scroll — no JavaScript

Scroll the panel. Each row fades, rises and unblurs as it enters view, and rewinds if you scroll back. There is no IntersectionObserver and no GSAP here — two lines of CSS do all of it.

The two new properties

  • animation-timeline: view() — instead of the default time-based timeline, drive this animation by the element's position within its scrollport. view() tracks an element entering and leaving view; scroll() tracks a scroll container's overall progress (perfect for a reading-progress bar).
  • animation-range — which slice of that journey the keyframes play over. entry 5% cover 35% means "start just as the element begins entering, finish a third of the way across." Tuning this range is how you control the feel.
Always provide a fallback. Scroll-driven animations are widely supported in modern browsers, but not universally. Wrap the animation in @supports (animation-timeline: view()), or — as in this component — use @supports not to force the content visible where it's missing. Never let a progressive enhancement hide your content from someone on an older browser. Same for prefers-reduced-motion: show it, don't move it.

When CSS, when GSAP ScrollTrigger?

Reach for native scroll-driven CSS for the common cases: reveal-on-scroll, progress bars, simple parallax. It's lighter and smoother than anything JS can do. Step up to GSAP's ScrollTrigger (Module 6) when you need pinning, complex multi-element timelines synced to one scroll, horizontal galleries, or fine programmatic control. They're complementary — a senior engineer uses native CSS for 80% and ScrollTrigger for the cinematic 20%.
