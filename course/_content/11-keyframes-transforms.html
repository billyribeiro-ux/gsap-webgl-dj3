A transition animates one change. A @keyframes animation lets you script every beat in between — 0%, 50%, 100% and anywhere else — and loop, delay or alternate it. Pair keyframes with the transform property and you have the entire CSS motion toolkit. And transform hides a superpower most people never use: real 3D.

Into the third dimension

Hover (or tap) the card. It rotates in 3D space to reveal its back — the classic flip. No library, no JavaScript animation; just three CSS properties working together.

The three keys to CSS 3D

  • perspective — set on the parent, it defines how far the viewer is from the screen. Smaller values (e.g. 600px) exaggerate the depth dramatically; larger ones are subtle. Without it, 3D rotations look flat and wrong.
  • transform-style: preserve-3d — tells an element that its children live in genuine 3D space, not flattened onto its own plane. The flip card's two faces need this to sit back-to-back.
  • backface-visibility: hidden — hides an element when its back is turned toward you. It's what stops you seeing a mirror-image of the front bleeding through during the flip.
Order matters in transforms. transform: rotateY(180deg) translateZ(40px) is not the same as the reverse — each operation happens in the coordinate space left by the previous one. Rotate-then-translate moves along the rotated axis; translate-then-rotate moves first, then spins. When a 3D transform looks "off," 90% of the time it's the order.

Keyframes for ambient motion

Beyond interaction, keyframes shine for ambient motion — the subtle, looping life that makes a page feel awake: a slow gradient drift, a gentle float, a pulsing glow. Use animation: float 6s ease-in-out infinite alternate and keep the movement tiny (a few pixels, a few degrees). The goal is a heartbeat you feel but never consciously notice. Combined with the 3D you just learned, a slowly rotating, floating product shot needs zero JavaScript.
