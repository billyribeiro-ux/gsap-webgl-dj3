The instant you need two things to happen in a deliberate order, manual delays become a nightmare — change one duration and you're re-tuning every delay after it by hand. A timeline solves this. You append tweens to a shared playhead, and GSAP keeps the timing relationships for you. It's the difference between juggling and conducting.

The position parameter — GSAP's secret weapon

Every .from() / .to() on a timeline takes an optional last argument that says where on the playhead it goes. This is the part that makes timelines feel like magic:

  • Nothing → append after the previous tween ends (the default).
  • '-=0.3' → start 0.3s before the previous one ends, overlapping them for a smoother, faster feel.
  • '<' → start at the same time as the previous tween began. '>' means at its end.
  • A label like 'reveal' → a named point on the timeline. Add tweens "at reveal" and they sync, no matter how earlier durations change. We even offset from it: 'reveal+=0.1'.
A timeline is a controllable object. Because the whole sequence lives on one playhead, you get tl.pause(), tl.reverse(), tl.timeScale(2) (play at double speed), tl.seek('reveal'), and tl.restart() — which is exactly what the replay button calls. You can even bind tl.progress() to a slider or, as you'll see next, to the scrollbar itself.

defaults: stop repeating yourself

Notice gsap.timeline({ defaults: { ease: 'expo.out', duration: 0.6 } }). Every tween added to that timeline inherits those values unless it overrides them. Set your house style once — your easing and base duration — and the whole sequence stays consistent. This is how teams keep dozens of animations feeling like one designer made them.
