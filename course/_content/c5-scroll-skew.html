"Smooth scroll" sites (think Locomotive, Lenis, Studio Freight) feel different the instant you touch the wheel: content lags and shears as if it has mass. The core idea is simple — measure how fast the user is scrolling and skew the content by that velocity, then ease the skew back to zero when they stop. We do it here without taking over native scrolling, so it stays accessible and works with the keyboard, trackpad and screen readers.

The two-value loop

  • Velocity. In a requestAnimationFrame loop, compare this frame's scrollTop to last frame's. The difference is velocity in pixels-per-frame — positive scrolling down, negative up.
  • A damped target. Turn velocity into a target skew (clamped so a fast flick can't tear the layout), then ease the current skew toward it: current += (target - current) * 0.1. That lerp is the inertia — the skew ramps up as you accelerate and glides back to flat as you stop.
  • Apply on the GPU. Write transform: skewY(…) (plus a hair of scaleY for a motion-blur stretch) to the content wrapper. transform-only, so it stays at 60fps no matter how long the list is.
Don't fight the scrollbar. Libraries that fully hijack scrolling can break keyboard navigation, focus, find-in-page and accessibility — use them deliberately, not by default. This velocity-skew approach is a read-only enhancement on top of native scroll: remove it and everything still works perfectly. Keep the maximum skew small (≈8–12°) and always disable it under prefers-reduced-motion; shearing content can be disorienting.

The same velocity signal can drive more than skew: scale media as you fling past it, intensify a shader's distortion with scroll speed, or tilt 3D objects. Velocity is one of the most expressive inputs you have — and now it's two numbers in a loop.
